*A memo on why the AI cycle is likely to end in consolidation, not collapse*

---

## The claim

This memo makes one argument. The AI cycle is more likely to end in consolidation than in a market-wide crash.

This is not one big bubble. It is many small ones. They are spread across thousands of smaller companies, and inflated by a story that a few large technology firms tell about the future.

When the small bubbles fail, the money does not fall out of the market in a crash. It moves up, to the handful of companies already at the top.

So the bubble implodes and consolidates. It does not explode and destroy. The result may be worse for everyone who is not already at the top.

---

## Why this is not 1999

The dot-com bubble was a stock bubble. People bought overpriced shares. The prices collapsed. The loss was spread across millions of small investors and pension funds. The damage was wide but shallow, and the lending system kept working.

Today is different. The companies at the center earn real money, and the numbers are clear.

Nvidia sells the core hardware. It reported about 216 billion USD in revenue for its 2026 fiscal year, up 65 percent in a year. Its net income was near 40 billion USD in a single quarter, on gross margins around 75 percent.
 
The pattern holds wider. The research firm DataTrek found that the five largest U.S. companies of 2025 have average net profit margins of about 34 percent. The five largest of 1999 had about 21 percent. Today's group also earns roughly 4.6 times the revenue and 9.1 times the net income of the 1999 group, after adjusting for inflation. These are real, profitable businesses, not ideas waiting for revenue.

Valuations are also far less stretched than they were then. In 1999 and early 2000, Cisco supplied the basic equipment for the internet build-out — the role Nvidia plays for AI today. Its price-to-earnings ratio reached the hundreds; estimates range from about 200 to over 470. It was worth more than USD 500 billion on roughly USD19 billion of revenue.

Nvidia today trades at about 32 times its past-year earnings, and near 21 times expected earnings. Goldman Sachs notes that the seven largest companies trade at about half the earnings multiple of the seven largest of the late 1990s. Their price-to-growth ratio is about 1.7, against 3.7 at the dot-com peak.

The companies that collapsed in 2000 often had no earnings at all. Amazon took in USD1.6 billion in revenue in 1999 but lost money, and reportedly had about ten months of cash left — yet it was valued at USD107 billion. Priceline reached a USD9.8 billion valuation with no route to profit. Pets.com and eToys went bankrupt. The Nasdaq fell more than 75 percent between 2000 and 2002 and erased over USD5 trillion in value.

Today's leaders are the opposite case. They have large profits and very little debt. Nvidia's debt-to-equity ratio is near 0.07. Their share prices are high, but they rest on real earnings.

Howard Marks draws a useful line in his [December 2025 memo](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/is-it-a-bubble).[^1] He writes that the question of an AI bubble actually holds "two different but interrelated bubble possibilities" — one in how the companies behave, and one in how investors price them. On the second, financial question, the leaders do not yet show the break from earnings that defined 1999.

Two cautions keep this honest. A few names, such as Tesla and Palantir, do trade at dot-com-style multiples. And Nvidia's price against sales, rather than earnings, is not far below Cisco's 2000 peak.

The market also leans on these few companies more than it leaned on the leaders of 1999. The five largest now make up about 30 percent of the S&P 500, the highest concentration in half a century. The leaders are solid. The market's dependence on them is the larger risk — and that is what the rest of this memo turns to.

---
## But it is not quite 2008 either

The closer comparison is the 2008 credit crisis. There, the danger sat in debt, and in who was lending to whom, rather than in share prices.

There are echoes of that here. The numbers come from mainstream financial institutions, not critics.

JPMorgan estimates that building out global data centers and AI infrastructure will cost more than USD5 trillion over five years. Even after the bond and securitization markets are tapped, it sees a funding gap of about USD1.4 trillion that private credit and governments will have to fill.[^2]

Much of the borrowing is already kept off the tech companies' own balance sheets. A Financial Times analysis found that Oracle, Meta, xAI, and CoreWeave have moved more than USD 120 billion of data center debt into special purpose vehicles. These are funded by private lenders such as Blue Owl, Pimco, BlackRock, and Apollo. The largest single example is Meta's USD 30 billion Hyperion deal in Louisiana, set up so that Meta rents the site rather than carrying the debt.[^3]

The risk is that this hides the true load. A UBS strategist quoted in that reporting warned that these structures mean the credit quality of the large tech companies could be worse than current models suggest. JPMorgan's own chief executive, Jamie Dimon, has said AI itself is not a bubble, but that the private credit market lacks the transparency of public markets.[^2]

Some of the demand is also circular. Nvidia agreed to invest up to USD 100 billion in OpenAI. OpenAI committed to huge cloud contracts with Oracle. Oracle buys its chips from Nvidia. So money can leave Nvidia as an investment and come back as revenue.[^4]

Fortune put the worry plainly. It asked how much of the AI boom is simply Nvidia's cash being recycled. It noted that in past bubbles, this kind of vendor financing and revenue "roundtripping" made the collapse worse.[^4]

Companies rarely disclose how much of their reported revenue comes from such related parties. That is part of why the SEC's Investor Advisory Committee recommended in December 2025 that the agency require clearer disclosure of AI-related "strategic dependencies."[^5]

The loans are often secured by the chips themselves. In one USD 20 billion financing for Elon Musk's xAI, a special purpose vehicle used up to USD 12.5 billion of debt to buy Nvidia chips and lease them back. The collateral is hardware whose value depends on the technology cycle staying favorable.[^3]

But there is a clear difference from 2008. The largest companies have steady cash flow from established businesses. Even if their AI bets disappoint, they can pay their debts from existing operations. A system-wide collapse would need their AI revenue to fail and their core businesses to weaken at the same time. That is possible, but it is two failures, not one.

So the largest companies are probably safe. The question is who is not.

---
## A field of small bubbles, not one large one

The risk is not in the giants. It sits in the layer beneath them.

That layer includes the AI startups spending through their venture funding. It includes mid-sized software firms that relabeled themselves "AI-first" and cut engineering teams to prove it. It includes data center developers in smaller markets, the regional banks that lent to them, and the private-equity-owned companies that borrowed on the bet that AI would cut their labor costs.

Each of these is a small bubble. On its own, none is big enough to threaten the system. Together they hold a large amount of wasted capital and a large number of jobs. When they deflate, the news will report that a large technology company beat its earnings — on the same day that thousands of people at smaller companies lose their jobs.

Technology has always worked this way to a degree. The venture model assumes most bets fail. What is different now is where the failures lead. And that depends on which kind of company fails.

Most AI startups sit in the application layer. They build products on top of someone else's model. When one fails, it leaves little behind: some code, perhaps a customer list, sometimes a team hired elsewhere. Most of its spending has already become revenue on a larger company's books.

A smaller number build the models themselves. These leave much more — trained models, research methods, datasets, and skilled people. But that residue tends to be absorbed by the largest companies, not recycled into new independent rivals.

That is the mechanism. Whether it takes the form of revenue, talent, or technology, the value created by failure moves toward the top.

---
## Consolidation up, not collapse down

Put those pieces together, and the prediction follows.

As the small bubbles deflate, their spending does not vanish in a crash. It has already moved up — to the companies that sell the core technology and own the infrastructure. The used equipment left behind can only be run at scale by those same companies.

So the cycle ends with the strong made stronger, and the field of challengers thinned out. The money moves up, not out. That is what it means to implode upward rather than collapse downward.

---

## Why this differs from the dot-com result

This is the part that matters most.

The dot-com crash was destructive, but it left useful things behind. The money spent on failed companies built cheap internet infrastructure, trained a generation of engineers, and lowered the cost of basic tools. That wreckage became the foundation for Amazon, Facebook, Stripe, and Uber. The failures paid for the next generation's start, and that generation was made of new companies.

This cycle runs the other way. The infrastructure being built is not shared ground that anyone can build on cheaply. It is owned — proprietary AI systems and the data centers that run them. When the failures clear, they do not leave cheap, shared tools for newcomers. They leave stronger incumbents.

The dot-com bust produced the next set of giants. This AI cycle may strengthen the giants we already have, and leave every new and smaller business dependent on them.

---

## The dependency problem

A software company in 2010 owned its own equipment: its servers, its code, its customer data. A software company built on AI today rents its core capability from one of a few large providers. That provider can raise prices, retire the product the company relies on, or release a competing one.

These newer companies are valued as if they own something durable. In practice, many are closer to renters. They own the storefront; someone else owns the recipe, and can change it. A large part of the economy risks becoming permanently dependent on a few suppliers.

---

## The effect on jobs

The labor market is one more channel for this concentration. Here the evidence is thinner, so the claim needs care.

The clearest effect is the one already described. When model-building companies fail or wind down, their best people are absorbed by the largest firms, not left to start rivals. For senior researchers and engineers, this is reabsorption, not unemployment. But it shows that even the labor market feeds talent upward, toward the incumbents.

The wider claim is harder to support, and should be held loosely. AI may give managers a reason for cuts they already wanted to make in a harder economy. The hiring that remains may concentrate into a few large companies. But the studies so far are early and mixed. They do not cleanly separate any AI effect from interest rates, the correction of post-2021 overhiring, and ordinary cost-cutting. That broad effect is plausible, but not yet established.

So the safe point is the narrow one. The same pull toward the top that shapes capital and technology also shapes where skilled people end up. Whether AI is hollowing out employment more widely is a real question, but not one this memo can settle.

---
## What would prove this wrong

The whole argument rests on one assumption: that companies have little real choice but to rent their core capability from a few large providers. If that stops being true, the case for consolidation weakens sharply.

The development most likely to make it untrue is the open-weight model — a model whose trained files a company can download and run on hardware it controls.

The difference comes down to how each option bills. A proprietary model reached through an interface is a meter that never stops. Every request is a payment. The provider sets the price, and can change or retire the product.

An open-weight model is the reverse. Once downloaded, it runs on rented servers or on a company's own machines. There is no per-request fee to the model's maker. The maker cannot raise the price or take the model away. And the data never has to leave the company.

For a business, this turns an open-ended rent into a fixed cost it controls. For regulated or privacy-sensitive work, it means sensitive data stays in the building.

Through early 2026, the practical case for this grew much stronger. Independent comparisons put the gap between the best open-weight models and the closed frontier at roughly six to nine months, and within a few points on common benchmarks. That is close enough that open models now handle most everyday work — summarizing documents, extracting data, running support agents, and routine coding.[^6]

Several of the strongest open models come from DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, and Google's Gemma. They carry permissive licenses that allow use on a company's own systems, offline if needed, with no licensing fee.[^6]

So on-premise use is real. But it is neither free nor universal. Running the largest open models still takes serious hardware; the biggest need several high-end chips. Full self-hosting therefore suits mid-sized and larger organizations more than the smallest. Smaller open models now run on a single graphics card, or even a capable laptop, which extends the option further down — though at lower capability.[^6]

The closed leaders also keep a real edge. They are ahead on the hardest tasks, on images and audio, and on the tools that make a model easy to build with. And self-hosting shifts a burden onto the company, which must now run, secure, and update the system itself.

There are also reasons the open path may not stay open. Vendor behavior is already splitting. Meta did the most to popularize open-weight models, but it paused new open releases in early 2026 and steered its best work toward a closed product. Today's open supplier can close tomorrow.[^7]

And much of the open-weight frontier is now driven by Chinese labs. That trades dependence on a few American providers for a different set of dependencies and policy risks. It does not remove dependence.

So the question is narrow and concrete. Does capability that is good enough for most work stay free and runnable on a company's own hardware? Or does the frontier pull far enough ahead, and stay closed enough, that renting it remains the only real choice?

If the open option holds, the value that failure pushes upward can flow outward again. New independent companies can build on it, the way they built on the cheap infrastructure left by the dot-com bust.

If it does not, the dependency described earlier hardens. The cycle then ends as a bust that mainly feeds the giants we already have.

Which way this resolves is the single most important thing to watch.

---

## Conclusion

This is not an argument that AI is fake. It is not a forecast of a 2008-style collapse. It is a forecast of something quieter.

A large amount of capital and labor is flowing into products that will fail. Because of how this cycle is built, the cost of those failures is being collected, in advance, by the companies at the top.

The bubble does not need to explode to do damage. It only needs to deflate slowly, while the wealth it created moves in one direction. The risk is not a crash. The risk is a more concentrated economy on the other side of it.
